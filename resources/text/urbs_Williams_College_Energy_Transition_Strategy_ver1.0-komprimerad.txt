This document is written to demonstrate a high-level strategy for
Williams College to undertake an energy transition to;
from the building-level infrastructure. 
The call for net-zero emissions is no longer limited to states & nations
and their commitments, it is very evident that businesses and large
institutions need to establish targets and create pathways.
Universities have a unique opportunity and a massive potential to
champion knowledge and showcase action within their communities,
both through sustainable physical infrastructure and encouraging
academic exploration in these areas. 
Each university has its set of challenges and to become truly net zero
requires institutional and operational structures addressing all
emissions. Within these challenges, energy efficiency, electrification,
and fuel switching for the built environment are relatively simple, bring
quantifiable and immediate emissions reductions. 
The strategy prepared by urbs together with Consigli looks specifically
to capture what we believe is the low-hanging fruit, in terms of
technology, deployment, and costs. This strategy focuses on an
enabling step based on proven and experiential knowledge from ‘the
Nordic’ countries that have had decades of development and leading
globally in fossil fuel-free heating infrastructures.

When planning an energy transition via either a change of fuel use or
increasing share of intermittent power from renewables, a key
challenge is to have enough capacity to cover the peak demands.
Understanding the peaks allows us to see how the energy can be
balanced within different user categories and over time. Thus, leading
to correctly plan for multiple energy sources under different scenarios
to achieve higher efficiency.
From our analysis, it is clear that a lot of energy is used in winter to fulfil
the need for heating. 
As an example, a much colder winter than normal could increase the
peak load significantly and pose multiple challenges to the energy
solution. Retaining some of the existing gas infrastructure would allow
for the right sizing of the infrastructure that can run without fossil
fuels under normal operations. 
Plotting of the peaks on a map demonstrates the heat rejection from
various locations, higher heating means more heat dissipating but less
obvious is heat getting rejected from the cooling production.
Cooling-related peaks during the summer are a thirrd of the winter's.
This is attributed to milder climates along with the summer holiday
season - reduced consumption of hot water and lighting energy.

The main challenge in the current system is the combustion-based hot
water production system, which is used to meet the heating and hot
water demands. 
Combustion-based heating solutions are inherently inefficient because
of the large amounts of wasted heat due to the cumulative
temperature differential between the combustion and ambient room
temperature. In addition, there are distribution losses associated with
an extensive supply network. Natural gas combustion also contributes
to air pollution and reduces air quality in the area by emitting nitrogen
oxides and sulfur dioxide. 
The key towards decarbonization is to shift away from combustion-
based systems. This would need capturing the energy, in the form of
heat, which is currently wasted. It is clear that the total heat rejected
during the course of the year is much more than the buildings actually
need. Heat loss associated with each stage of energy production,
supply, distribution, and at the end of use, makes the current system
inefficient.

and can be captured to reduce the heating production and the peaks;

OUR APPROACH
A DECENTRALIZED SYSTEM
The current system and its challenges;
Our experiential knowledge of creating 5th-generation district
energy solutions; and 
The current technologies available and our insight into upcoming
technologies.
The current infrastructure on campus relies on a centralized system
where heating and cooling to the buildings are supplied from siloed and
centralized plants. This creates inefficiencies as both systems work
independently and against one another, requiring more energy and
emitting more GHG emissions. Additionally, the reliance on fossil-fuel
sources creates both an increase in emissions upstream and
downstream. 
Our approach is based on our understanding of:
Since the campus is an extensive network with multiple types of
buildings, that vary in size, quality, year of build, and a variety of energy
distribution systems and types of use, a decentralized system should
be established by creating various energy nodes. A decentralized
solution would allow us to maximize energy capture and use it as close
to the consumption as possible, it will also allow us to limit excess
energy production. This will lead to a reduction in energy demand,
distribution, and production.
Our proposal is to create a decentralized system on campus that
utilizes simultaneous heating and cooling and works towards balancing
the system. 
PAGE 8 |  STRATEGY FOR ENERGY TRANSITION
Each 'energy node' would target a group of buildings and connect them
via various appropriate alternatives namely: thermal loop, energy
storage, smart controls, etc., using existing infrastructure as much as
possible. By creating an energy node that can balance itself,
infrastructure interjections can be focused on capturing waste as
opposed to energy production. The opportunity lies in capturing all the
waste heat, given the high amount of heat generation and consumption
in the system. Recapturing heat at the point of exhaust and re-using it
as close to the demand as possible is one of the most efficient
solutions. 
Each node could have multiple common solutions including, but not
limited to short-term, long-term, and seasonal energy storage in form
of geothermal boreholes to store the heat rejected from cooling to be
used during the peak winter season, which would reduce the primary
energy need for heating. Furthermore, each building within the node is
retrofitted with heat recovery ventilation. By reviewing the campus
energy profile, we have understood that the size of the new
infrastructure should be limited to capture the waste energy. Thus, the
size of the boreholes should be limited to meeting the cooling demand
or capturing the heat rejected. 
This will make the new infrastructure interjections spatially,
economically, and technically efficient. We are not looking to create a
1-to-1 energy production infrastructure. Using systems thinking and
taking a holistic view of the whole infrastructure, we can create an
efficient system that recovers, re-uses, and recycles energy inputting
more energy into the system.

EXPERIENTIAL LEARNING
NODE 1 AS A LIVING LAB
The strategy is a physical manifestation of the commitments of
Williams College, the state of Massachusetts, Consigli, and urbs to
identify, develop, and deploy climate change solutions that are
informed by research, abreast with the latest technology, and
encouraged by the open exchange of ideas. At its core, the physical
plan is designed to reflect the legacy of the college, and demonstrate
strategies to mitigate and adapt to the impacts of climate change.
The goal of Node 1 would be to create and foster a scalable
infrastructure model for the rest of the campus. This would be the first
phase in achieving a decentralized, post-fossil, and net-zero campus. 
Node 1 will act as both a testbed and showcase of the most innovative
and effective sustainability solutions for Williams College and set the
standard for the following nodes. It would represent a new model that
empowers open exchange and collaboration for sustainable solutions
to climate change for Massachusetts, the USA, and the world.

As climate targets quickly approach, regional, national, and global
policies and efforts are becoming more aggressive to meet these
deadlines. In order to limit global temperatures to below 1.5°C of pre-
industrial levels, electrification coupled with the energy transition will
be necessary processes on all scales.  
The energy transition shifts away from fossil-based sources to
renewable and lower-carbon sources. By 2050, 90% of global
electricity can come from renewable sources, exponentially reducing
emissions from the electricity sector while simultaneously driving
development and green jobs. Within Massachusetts, there are targets
of decreasing emissions from electric power by 53% in 2025, and 70%
by 2030.
To ensure Williams College can take advantage of this energy
transition, electrification will be the key to achieving a post-fossil
campus. Electrification offers more efficient systems that require less
primary energy, emit less GHG emissions, and decrease operational
expenditures.

THE FUTURE OF WILLIAMS COLLEGE
PAGE 16 | STRATEGY FOR ENERGY TRANSITION
Universities and colleges stand at the crossroads of students'
academic and professional lives. They provide opportunities to drive
behavioural change through partnerships with private and public actors.
Net-zero initiatives, such as this one, open up opportunities for
campuses to amplify their sustainability knowledge and expertise in
technological, social, and policy-making spheres of the wider
community.
As a living sustainability lab, the college can test solutions at scale
within the campus boundaries. The resulting innovations, learnings, and
changes can then be deployed into the community to achieve greater
scale. Simultaneously, education and training on climate change, driven
by the college can strengthen the leaders of today and prepare leaders
for tomorrow. The professors and students on campus can co-create
net zero initiatives and ensure the positive and beneficial impacts of
these initiatives. 
The future of Williams College is one that exemplifies the sustainability
principles which it stands for, through both intangible assets, in the
form of academic curriculums and future leaders, and through their
tangible assets such as campus infrastructure.
