The district heating transition
The industry is continually transitioning from first-generation oil-fired district heating to today’s fourth-generation intelligent systems. Throughout this journey, we have played a significant role in deploying some of the most advanced and efficient networks globally.

This acts to reduce reliance on fossil fuels and integrates renewable and waste surplus heat by lowering grid temperatures.

Ultimately, the ambition is to deliver systems that consistently meet demand while minimizing wasted heat and energy—supplying exactly what is required with the lowest possible environmental impact.
District Heating 1st Gen – 4th Gen
[notes] The 4 Generations of District Heating
Introduction "To understand where we are going, we must understand where we’ve come from. The history of district heating is defined by a continuous drive towards lower temperatures and higher efficiency. We categorize this evolution into four distinct generations.“

1st Generation (1880–1930): The Steam Age
The System: This was the era of steam. Systems used steam pipes in concrete ducts to transport heat at very high temperatures—often up to 200°C.
The Logic: Steam was chosen because early systems primarily served industry and large complexes like hospitals that required high heat content with low mass flows.
The Drawback: While effective for its time, it was inefficient due to high heat losses and dangerous due to the high pressures involved.

2nd Generation (1930–1980): The Pressurized Water Age
The Shift: The major leap forward here was the transition from steam to pressurized superheated water, delivering heat at temperatures above 100°C.
The Driver: This generation was driven by the need for operational safety and better efficiency. It unlocked the potential of Combined Heat and Power (CHP) plants.
The Impact: By utilizing waste heat from power plants (rather than just burning coal/oil for heat alone), this generation reduced fossil fuel consumption by roughly 50% compared to separate production.

3rd Generation (1980–2020): The Prefabricated Age
Current Standard: This is what many of us see in the ground today. It introduced "lean" components—pre-insulated pipes and compact substations manufactured off-site.
The Temperature: We moved generally below 100°C for the first time. Lower temperatures meant significantly reduced heat losses and lower investment costs.
The Mix: This era began the shift away from pure fossil fuels, integrating biomass and some industrial surplus heat into the grid.

4th Generation (2020–2050): The Smart Energy Age
The Future: We are now transitioning to 4th Generation District Heating (4GDH). The defining characteristic is the ultra-low flow temperature of 50–60°C.
Why Low Temp? Lower grid temperatures are essential for integrating fluctuating renewable energy sources like wind, solar, and low-grade waste heat from data centers.
The System: It is no longer just a one-way delivery system; it is a smart, two-way grid where buildings can interact with the network (e.g., as prosumers). It focuses on minimizing heat loss and integrating the heating sector with the electricity grid for total energy system efficiency.Supermarket – Occupants consume heat, food storage refrigerators take away heat to the networkBiomass – Forward direction is to produce heat from biomass, reverse direction is to produce heat from rejected heat using heat pump
Conclusion "In summary, the journey from 1st to 4th generation is a journey from high-temperature steam to low-temperature intelligent water systems. Each step has allowed us to reduce waste, integrate greener energy sources, and lower the environmental impact of heating our cities."

What is a heat pump?
A heat pump is a device that moves heat from one place to another using electricity. It does not create heat like a boiler, it only transfers heat.
It takes heat from a colder place (like air, water, or ground) and delivers it to a warmer place (like a building) 
Types of heat pump:
Air to air (home, offices)
Air to water (buildings with radiators, domestic hot water)
Water to water (large scale district heating)
[notes] Speaker Notes:
Simple definition: moves heat, doesn’t create it.
Explain types with examples.
Position as bridge between low-grade heat and usable heat.


--- slide 25 ---

How does a heat pump work?
Heat pump components:
A heat pump uses electricity to drive a compressor and move heat from a colder source (air, ground, water, wastewater, data center cooling water) to a warmer sink (heating system / district heating).
Evaporator: absorbs low‑temperature heat from the source. (a-b)
Compressor: raises the pressure and temperature of the refrigerant (b-c)
Condenser: releases heat at a useful temperature into the district network (c-d)
Expansion: process where the refrigerant pressure and temperature drop (d-a)
Why can it deliver more heat than the electricity it consumes?
Because most of the delivered heat comes from the source, not from the electricity itself.
[notes] Speaker Notes:
Explain cycle: evaporator → compressor → condenser → expansion.
Key insight: majority of heat comes from source, not electricity.

How is the waste heat reused?
Source: Equinix
[notes] Speaker Notes:
This slide shows a typical heat recovery pathway from a data centre.
Waste heat from servers is first captured via the cooling system—often liquid cooling loops.
This heat is then transferred through heat exchangers into a secondary circuit.
A heat pump is typically used to upgrade the temperature to usable levels.
The upgraded heat is then distributed to: 
District heating networks
Buildings
Industrial processes
The key concept here is closed-loop reuse, where heat is not rejected but reintegrated into the energy system.

--- slide 32 ---

Heat Reuse Configurations
Ambient: Waste heat is transferred to a low‑temperature ambient loop shared between buildings.
Centralised: Waste heat is sent to a central plant where heat pumps upgrade it for district heating supply.
Hybrid ambient / centralized: Waste heat is partly shared via an ambient loop and partly boosted centrally to higher temperatures.
Chip heating: Heat is recovered directly at server/chip level for very efficient low‑temperature reuse.
Indirect: Waste heat is transferred via an intermediate loop or heat exchanger before being reused.
Source: The Trade Council of Denmark, International District Energy Association and New York State of Opportunity

--- slide 33 ---

Ambient
Source: The Trade Council of Denmark, International District Energy Association and New York State of Opportunity
[notes] Speaker Notes:
In an ambient system, heat is shared via a low-temperature loop, typically 20–40°C.
Buildings connected to the loop use local heat pumps to extract or reject heat.
This allows: 
Simultaneous heating and cooling
High flexibility
Data centres act as continuous heat sources within the loop.
This configuration is ideal for 5th generation district energy systems.


--- slide 34 ---

Centralised
Source: The Trade Council of Denmark, International District Energy Association and New York State of Opportunity
[notes] Speaker Notes:
In a centralised system, heat from the data centre is transported to a central energy plant.
Large heat pumps are used to upgrade the temperature to district heating supply levels.
Advantages: 
Easier operation and control
Economies of scale
Suitable for existing district heating networks where higher temperatures are required.


--- slide 35 ---

Hybrid Ambient / Centralised
Source: The Trade Council of Denmark, International District Energy Association and New York State of Opportunity
[notes] Speaker Notes:
A hybrid system combines both ambient and centralised approaches.
Part of the heat is shared directly via a low-temperature loop.
The remaining heat is upgraded centrally.
This provides: 
Greater flexibility
Improved efficiency
Better optimisation of load profiles
Increasingly, this is seen as the most adaptable solution.


--- slide 36 ---

Chip Heating
Source: The Trade Council of Denmark, International District Energy Association and New York State of Opportunity
[notes] Speaker Notes:
This approach captures heat directly at the server or chip level.
Because the heat is captured closer to the source: 
Higher temperatures are achievable
Losses are minimized
This improves reuse potential significantly.
However, it requires: 
Advanced cooling systems
Higher integration complexity
This is particularly relevant for AI and high-density computing systems.


--- slide 37 ---

Indirect
Source: The Trade Council of Denmark, International District Energy Association and New York State of Opportunity
[notes] Speaker Notes:
In an indirect system, heat is transferred via an intermediate loop.
This typically involves heat exchangers separating the data centre and district systems.
Advantages: 
Improved safety and reliability
Easier retrofitting
Common in cases where systems must remain hydraulically or operationally isolated.

Unlocking data center waste heat for low-carbon campus heating in the UK

A campus-wide transition to low-carbon heating integrating data center heat recovery and high-efficiency heat pump systems to support an existing district heating network at a University in the UK.

The challenge
Peak load of ~11,000 kW across campus 
Integrating new heat pumps into an existing network 
Recovering waste heat from data center loads 
Ensuring stable and reliable operation

The solution
Replacement of conventional plant (CHP, chillers, boilers) with heat pump-based solutions
Use of shunt pump arrangements to ensure stable operation and temperature control
Booster pump system enabling controlled injection of heat into the district heating circuit at target temperature
The outcome
Integration of waste heat into district heating supply at up to 75 °C
Operating temperature levels: Evaporator: 25-16 °C and Condenser: 60-75 °C
Reduced dependency on gas-based CHP and boilers 
Improved system stability through storage and controlled pump operation 
Scalable architecture enabling phased expansion across campus buildings

Powering 6,000 homes by reusing waste heat from a data center in Denmark

A large-scale heat reuse initiative in Høje Taastrup integrates a Hyperscaler data center’s waste heat into the district heating network, supporting decarbonization goals while improving energy utilization efficiency across the system.

The challenge
Large-scale integration of data center waste heat into district heating
Managing low-grade heat (approx. 30/15°C) requiring temperature lift
Balancing heat supply vs. seasonal demand fluctuations
Ensuring reliable heat delivery to a wide residential base

The solution
Deployment of waste heat recovery system at the data center interface
Installation of heat exchangers & heat pump to upgrade temperature
Integration with existing district heating and cooling energy station
Use of centralized thermal storage (pit pond storage) to balance supply and demand
The outcome
33,000 MWh large-scale pit thermal storage for heat buffering
Approximate data center temperature levels: 30°C supply / 15°C return
Heat upgraded using large heat pump systems before grid injection
System connected to an existing combined district heating/cooling plant
