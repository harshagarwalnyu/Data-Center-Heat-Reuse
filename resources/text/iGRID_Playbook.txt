Introduction to
District Energy (DE)


--- page 5 ---
District energy systems centralize the production of heating or cooling (hot water or 
chilled water) and use pumping systems for distributing it to multiple connected 
buildings, through a network of insulated pipes.
The connected buildings, also known as connected consumers, can be of various types 
such as:
• Residential Buildings: Homes and apartments
• Commercial Buildings: Offices, shops, hotels, leisure retail 
and other commercial establishments
• Public Buildings: Schools, hospitals, transport & infrastructure, 
and other public buildings
• Industrial Facilities: For both comfort cooling/heating
as well as process cooling/heating
These district energy networks/grids are operated by specialist companies, known as 
district energy utilities. These utility companies can have different ownership and 
operational structures: government, semi-government, private, public, municipal, 
residential cohort etc.
The connected consumers typically pay a monthly bill for their consumed heating or 
cooling energy, which is measured by the means of an energy meter (BTU meter) to 
these district energy utilities. This allows the district energy utilities to operate the 
networks for profit. Exceptions to this exist in certain countries (e.g., Denmark), where 
the utility companies are not allowed to operate these networks for a profit.

In certain cases, the central heating/cooling plant is owned by the same owner as the 
buildings that it may be connected to. As an example, a central heating and cooling 
plant on an educational university campus, airport, hospital complex etc. In such cases, 
a billing system may not be deployed. These systems are also referred to as campus 
heating/cooling networks.
We can therefore generalize by saying: district heating/cooling networks supply energy 
to several connected buildings, where the ownership of the network and buildings is 
different and campus heating/cooling systems supply energy to several connected 
buildings under where the ownership of the network and buildings is common. 
A district energy and campus energy system are fundamentally the same, with same 
design and operational principles applying to both. Hence, it’s important to clearly 
define how we in Grundfos define the District Energy Vertical.
The connection at the connected consumer can vary between more commonly used 
indirect connection (using a heat exchanger), or by a less commonly used direct 
connection (where the water from the district energy grid is pumped directly until the 
final connected load). 
DE Introduction
In Grundfos, the District Energy Vertical covers district energy networks 
and campus energy networks - only when the campus energy network 
is considered, designed, and constructed as its own separate 
project/tender, different to the building(s) that it will be connected to. 
In Grundfos, the District Energy Vertical covers the scope of the entire 
grid, until the point which falls under the scope (design, supply & 
operation) of the District Energy utility/operator and from where the 
connected consumer scope starts.
This distinction is made as the vertical approach of specialization will allow us to focus 
our competencies and solution selling approach to the specific stakeholders that will be 
involved in the campus energy project.
If the campus heating/cooling plant is considered under the same project tender of the 
buildings that it is being connected to (therefore being handled by the same designer, 
contractor, installer, and other stakeholders) we would define such a plant under the 
vertical that best defines the connected building(s) e.g., CBS Education, CBS Transport & 
Infrastructure, CBS Healthcare, CBS Retail etc.

DE Introduction
As district energy systems utilize pumping systems to distribute the energy, 
this represents an opportunity of significant interest to Grundfos. District 
energy networks can also be quite varied in size: 3.5 MW – 5,500 MW for 
District Heating, 3,000 RT – 150,000 RT for District Cooling
District heating
• As per research, in 2022, district heating systems supplied approximately 9% of the global final 
heating need in buildings and industry.
• In Europe (EU), approximately 12% of all heating and hot water needs are met via district heating.
• District heating currently represents around 4% of global CO2 emissions.
• China (38% of all heating) and Russia (45% of all heating) are the largest contributors to district 
heat production globally.
• Fossil fuels still dominate the district heating production globally (about 90% of total heat 
production).
• By 2040 it’s expected that 40% of district heating will be done with renewable energy sources or by 
use of heat from waste hear sources.
• District heating is expected to grow modestly with a 4% growth rate, year over year until 2030
Cities worldwide are actively pursuing an energy transition with a focus on heating and cooling. Over 700 
cities, across 53 countries have made commitments to halve their emissions by 2030 and achieve net zero 
emissions by 2050 (cities race to zero).
Together, all heating and cooling systems account for nearly half of the world's energy consumption. Today, 
most of this energy comes from fossil fuels, contributing significantly to greenhouse gas emissions and air 
pollution. 
By 2040, it is expected that more than 850 million people will move into cities from rural areas & smaller 
towns, adding to an already expanding energy need within cities. Regulatory requirements and political 
demands require that cities take actions to meet these increasing cooling and heating demands in a 
sustainable way.  
By transitioning to renewable-based and energy-efficient heating and cooling systems, cities can reduce their 
environmental footprint, achieve their sustainability targets, and combat climate change.
District energy systems allow the integration of renewable energy sources, use waste heat and are 
the most scalable energy efficient way of heating and cooling spaces. Therefore, district energy aligns 
perfectly with the energy transition ambitions set out by cities, and it is therefore we see more 
governments regulating towards mandatory or incentivized use of District Energy.
District cooling
• It is approximated that as per 2022, around 8% of all cooling needs globally were met using district 
cooling.
• In the Middle East, district cooling accounts for approximately 20% of all cooling needs. In Europe 
(EU) district cooling accounts for about 5% of all cooling needs (Sweden 18.4%, Finland 14.8%, 
France, 11.2%).
• It is expected that by 2050, 2/3rd of all households and buildings will require cooling.
• Cooling systems contribute to 50-70% of peak electricity demand, and it is estimated that 20% of 
world’s electricity consumption, and this is expected to increase to approximately 40% of world’s 
total electricity consumption by 2050.
• District cooling can improve the efficiency of cooling by 5-10 times compared to conventional 
unitary systems, and 1.5-3 times compared to stand-alone chilled water systems.
• District cooling is expected to grow at a rate of 7-9%, year over year until 2030.

1. Deep Decarbonization Goals
•
Zero-Emission Targets: With the global focus on mitigating climate change, district heating systems 
will increasingly aim for zero-emission operations. This will involve a rapid shift away from fossil fuels 
such as coal and natural gas towards renewable energy sources.
•
Renewable Heat Expansion: Expect a significant increase in the integration of renewable energy 
sources like solar, wind, geothermal, and biomass. Governments and municipalities will incentivize 
and mandate the adoption of these clean energy technologies.
2. Energy Efficiency Enhancements
•
Advanced Heat Pumps: The deployment of more efficient and advanced heat pump technologies will 
become prevalent. These systems will be able to extract heat from various sources, including the air, 
water, and ground, with higher efficiency rates.
•
Building Retrofits: There will be a strong emphasis on improving the energy efficiency of buildings 
connected to district heating systems. Retrofitting programs will be widespread, optimizing heat 
retention and reducing overall demand.
3. Digitalization and Smart Grid Integration
•
AI and Predictive Analytics: Expect the widespread adoption of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine 
learning algorithms in district heating operations. These technologies will optimize energy 
production, distribution, and consumption based on real-time data.
•
Blockchain for Energy Trading: Blockchain technology will likely be used to facilitate peer-to-peer 
energy trading within district heating networks. Consumers can buy, sell, and exchange excess heat or 
electricity efficiently and securely.
4. Hybrid and Multi-Energy Systems
•
Hybrid District Heating Systems: District heating networks will increasingly integrate with other 
energy systems, such as electricity and cooling networks. This will create multi-energy systems that 
offer greater flexibility and resilience.
•
Power-to-Heat Solutions: Excess renewable electricity will be converted into heat through power-to-
heat technologies. This approach helps balance the grid, store energy, and meet heating demands 
during peak times.
5. Circular Economy Principles
•
Waste Heat Recovery: Industries and data centers will play a larger role in district heating, with 
systems designed to capture and utilize waste heat. This contributes to the circular economy by 
reducing waste and maximizing resource efficiency.
•
Biogas and Biofuels Integration: The use of biogas from organic waste and biofuels derived from 
agricultural residues will increase. These bio-based fuels will serve as sustainable alternatives to 
traditional fossil fuels.
History and Trends
District heating has evolved through four generations. The 1st generation used steam at high 
temperatures (up to 200°C) for heating. The 2nd generation transitioned to pressurized hot water 
above 100°C, improving efficiency and safety. The 3rd generation introduced lower temperatures 
(below 100°C) and pre-insulated pipes, enhancing energy efficiency and reducing costs .The 4th 
generation focuses on sustainability, integrating renewable energy sources and operating at even 
lower temperatures (below 70°C) to minimize carbon emissions.

•
Environmental & sustainability goals: District energy systems can be significantly more 
energy-efficient than individual heating or cooling systems in buildings. This is because 
they utilize larger equipment which is typically more efficient and is operated by specialists 
with a focus to make production and distribution as efficient as possible.
•
Lack of skilled labor: Lack of competent professionals required to operate and maintain 
individual building heating and cooling systems, also makes for a strong case for 
centralized production of heating and cooling, operated by specialists. Most individualized 
heating and cooling systems never operate as per design or efficiency intent. As district 
energy operators are incentivized to efficient operation, there is a very large focus on 
continuous optimization of the grid making for a more efficient operation. 
•
Sector coupling: Sector coupling refers to the concept of connecting different energy 
sectors, traditionally electricity/power generation and heating/cooling (buildings) into an 
integrated system The goal is to optimize energy use across these sectors and achieve a 
more sustainable energy system, particularly by leveraging renewable energy sources.
District energy can utilize waste heat from industrial processes, data centers or co-
generation plants (CHP) that produce both electricity and heat. By reducing reliance on 
individual fossil fuel burning systems, district energy contributes to lower greenhouse gas 
emissions and air pollution.
•
Integration of Renewables: District energy systems can be designed to incorporate 
renewable energy sources like biomass, geothermal, or solar thermal to supply heat. This 
flexibility allows them to adapt to the growing trend towards a more sustainable energy 
mix.
District energy offers a compelling approach to sustainable energy use, but there are some 
hurdles to its widespread adoption:
•
High Upfront Costs: Installing a district energy system requires significant upfront 
investment for infrastructure like pipes, pumping stations, and central heating/cooling 
plants. This can be a deterrent for developers and municipalities, especially compared to the 
lower initial costs of individual building systems.
•
Lack of Awareness and Expertise: Compared to traditional energy systems, district energy is 
a relatively complex concept.  A lack of public awareness and expertise in planning, 
designing, and operating these systems can hinder project development.
•
Split Incentives and Long-Term Commitment: Building owners may be hesitant to connect 
to a district system if they perceive a loss of control over their energy use or costs. 
Additionally, the long-term commitment required for district energy projects can be a 
challenge, especially for rapidly developing areas.

•
Government Support: Many governments are actively promoting district energy to achieve 
their environmental sustainability goals. This support can come in the form of subsidies, tax 
breaks, or favorable regulations that make district energy more attractive to developers and 
property owners.
•
Urbanization and Population Growth: As cities grow and populations become denser, the 
demand for energy increases. District energy systems offer a scalable solution to meet these 
growing needs in a more sustainable way compared to traditional individual building 
systems. It is estimated that more than 850 million people will move into cities, increasing 
the demand for heating and cooling in already very densely populated areas, making DE a 
scalable solution of choice.
•
Modernization and Infrastructure Upgrades: In many areas, existing district energy systems 
are being modernized with improved efficiency, reduced heat losses, and the ability to 
accommodate new heat sources like renewable energy.
•
Resilience and Reliability: District energy networks can provide increased energy resilience, 
especially when designed with multiple heat sources and thermal energy storage. This 
ensures a more reliable supply of energy during extreme weather events or disruptions in 
the traditional energy grid.
•
Retrofitting Challenges: Integrating existing buildings into a new district energy system can 
be complex and expensive. Buildings may require modifications to their heating and 
cooling systems, adding to the overall project cost.
•
Regulatory Uncertainty: The regulatory environment for district energy can be unclear in 
some regions. Uncertainties around pricing structures, permitting processes, and allocation 
of responsibility can discourage potential investors.
•
Customer Perception: Public perception of district energy systems can be a barrier. 
Concerns about reliability, affordability, and potential disruption during construction can 
make it difficult to gain public support for new projects.
Despite these challenges, there are ongoing efforts to address these barriers and promote 
the benefits of district energy. Government incentives, public-private partnerships, public 
education initiatives, and the development of standardized design and contracting 
approaches are all helping to pave the way for wider adoption of district energy.

Market Challenges
High Capital Expenditure (CAPEX) 
•
New district energy systems are expensive to develop, making 
it difficult for local authorities to raise capital or attract private 
interest without securing customers first. The high upfront 
costs include building infrastructure and associated 
development costs such as permitting and feasibility studies
•
This requires projects to have a large subscriber base 
to achieve economies of scale
Operational and Pricing Issues
•
Lack of awareness about benefits and cost savings, inadequate 
infrastructure and planning, and insufficient knowledge to attract 
investments
•
Grid access limitations, pricing regimes that disadvantage district 
energy systems vis-à-vis other energy systems and issues related 
to heat metering are also challenges in the current context
•
Additionally, there are sometimes high operating costs - with 
charges for metering and billing in some cases exceeding the 
actual energy costs 
Competition from individual heating solutions
•
Individual heating solutions for homes (including usage of 
small heat pumps) and cost efficiency of coal-based and 
natural gas-based heating solutions in some regions present 
a challenge to district energy
Lack of Skilled Workforce
•
One of the challenges with establishing district energy systems 
can be a lack of a skilled workforce - particularly workers who can 
integrate low/zero-carbon technologies into new or existing 
systems
Sources: IEA, District Energy Initiative, Link1, primary research and expert interviews

•

Circulating Pump Market: Europe        (1/4)
• Currently, ~67 million EU citizens are directly supplied by district heating and cooling – with ~140 million EU citizens live in a city already equipped with a district 
heating and cooling infrastructure. 
• Europe’s district heating and cooling network infrastructure comprises ~190,000 km of district heating pipes across the continent. Some networks are more than 40 
years old, with aged equipment and low level of digitalisation. District cooling networks, in comparison, are less than 2,000 km across Europe
– As per Euroheat, EUR 144 billion investment is required for district heating networks by 2030 in Europe – to move towards 100% renewable and climate-neutral 
district heating and cooling networks by 2050 – as laid out in the ‘Fitfor55’ package
– Expanding district heating and cooling networks can contribute to achieving ~20% of the EU heat demand by 2030 and ~50% by 2050. Using district energy can 
also save 24 billion cubic metres (bcm) of gas demand (6 bcm – through modernization of the network and 18 bcm through the phase-in of clean and renewable 
energy sources)
– There are currently over 17,000 heat/cold networks in Europe, representing only ~13% of Europe’s heating demand
Sources: EuroHeat, International District Energy Association, IREES, Link, Primary research and expert interviews

North Europe – in Scandinavian countries like Denmark, Finland and Sweden DH share of space heating is more than 50%
• District cooling has limited adoption and grid integration for residential use in the region – it is primarily used to cater to commercial cooling requirements. 
District cooling market is more prevalent in markets like Sweden and France
o Paris has a large cooling network that operates under a concession model called ‘Climespace’. This network is the largest district cooling network in Europe 
– with a cooling capacity of 215 MW, and ~570 customers, including the Louvre museum, Forum des Halles as well as many restaurants, hotels and shops
• District heating contributes to ~85% of the circulating pumps market by value – while cooling contributes ~5%. The combined cooling and heating application 
contributes ~10% of the market
• Variable-speed drives and intelligent controls are being used extensively in the region for optimising pump performance and reducing energy consumption
• Heating/cooling plants in Europe typically use large centrifugal pumps (500-2000kW) for primary circulation, especially in larger systems with significant heating 
or cooling demands – while sub-stations use secondary pumps (20-400 kW) depending on the system design and distribution network complexity. Smaller 
pumps (up to 50 kW) are mostly used in the building interface. In the large/small pumping stations, the pump sizes are in the 20-300 kW range
• Sub-station accounts for the greatest pump usage in district heating (30% by value) and combined application (40% by value) to regulate water temperature and 
manage secondary water flow, while for district cooling, majority of the pumps are installed in the production side, leading to ~72% share by value in the cooling 
plant

Circulating Pump Market: Finland  (2/2)
Market Insights
• District heating (DH) remains a popular choice in Finland – with ~65% of new buildings choosing DH to meet heating requirements. 
Currently, DH caters to nearly half of Finnish homes and commercial buildings. In some urban areas, district heating covers more than

• About 70% of Finland’s municipalities produce DH mainly with renewable energy sources or waste heat. Further, carbon emissions from

• Finland also has Europe's third largest combined heating and cooling system – The Katri Vala heat pump plant produces 90 MW of 
district heating and 60 MW of district cooling – to power the heat energy needs of 250,000 Helsinki residents and the cooling needs of 
100 large commercial properties.
• Individual heat pump adoption is also on the rise in Finland, which may act as a barrier for the district energy market in the future
• District heating accounts for ~87% of the pump market, due to the extreme cold weather conditions in the Nordics region.  Combined application share 
of the pumps market is ~4%, while district cooling accounts for 9% of the market
• Circulating pump market expected to grow at a CAGR of 9.41% in value terms to reach EUR 59.75 million by 2028
• Large/Small pumping stations account for the largest share of the district heating by value, accounting for ~30% of the market – to meet the pumping 
needs of a well-connected district energy network (both residential and commercial buildings)

especially in North China, where there are more than 100 cities with access to district heating networks
•
Cooling application is prominent in regions in Southern China
•
District heating, in value terms, accounted for ~89% of the overall pump market while cooling contributed ~6%. Combined application share was

Applications in District Heating
Main process pumps: 
These are the large, centralized 
pumps, that are pumping across 
the heat source to produce the hot 
or chilled water and carry out the 
primary distribution across the 
network.
Sub-stations:
These are pumping stations across 
the network, that are placed to 
meet the temperature and/or 
pressure requirements in specific 
zones within the networks.
Ancillary water systems:
These are pumping systems 
placed within the main central 
plant as well as substations, that 
are supportive for the proper 
operation of the main process 
such as:  make-up water pump 
systems, system pressurization 
etc.
Water quality systems:
As district energy networks are 
large recirculation systems, it’s 
important to ensure water quality 
will not have detrimental effects 
to the equipment at the central 
plant, connected consumers of 
within the network. Water quality 
management is ensured by the 
use of filtration systems, de-
gassing systems and chemical 
dosing & disinfection systems. 
POE (point of entry) water 
treatment is an increasingly 
important application, as use of 
fresh water for make-up is getting 
prohibited through legislation in 
many parts of the world.
Wastewater:
Drainage pumps to handle 
potential leakage/flooding that 
can occur within the plant and 
substations.

The Grundfos iGRID Concept
Low-Temperature Zones: 
iGRID divides district heating grids 
into smaller zones with similar 
building characteristics. This 
allows for more precise 
temperature control, ensuring 
that the entire grid doesn’t have to 
operate at the highest 
temperature demand.
Real-Time Data:
The system uses real-time data to 
adjust pump speed and optimize 
heat distribution according to 
actual demand and weather 
conditions. This helps in reducing 
heat loss and improving overall 
efficiency.
Demand-Driven Distribution: 
By focusing on demand-driven 
distribution, iGRID ensures that 
pressure is only added when 
needed, which reduces thermal 
stress on pipes and components, 
thereby extending their lifespan.
Integration of Renewable 
Energy: 
The low-temperature zones make 
it easier to integrate renewable 
energy sources like wind and solar 
into the district heating system.
Heat Loss Reduction:
By optimizing supply 
temperatures and using 
decentralised mixing loops, iGRID 
can reduce heat loss by up to 30%.
Here are the key features:
Grundfos iGRID range of solutions have been designed to optimize district heating 
systems by creating intelligent, low-temperature zones and using real-time data. Zoning 
of the grid enables utilisation of local low temperature heat sources and reducing heat 
loss. 
Overall, Grundfos iGRID aims to make district heating systems more 
efficient, sustainable, and capable of integrating renewable energy sources

iGRID Temperature Zone – Overview And Benefits
The iGRID Temperature Zone is a prefabricated mixing 
loop supplied ready for installation.
To regulate the temperature in the controlled zone, it 
mixes the colder return flow into the hot supply flow.
iGRID Temperature Zone solution comprises of:
1. Bypass connecting return to supply line
2.Variable speed mixing pump controlled by the 
temperature in supply to the zone
3.Sensors (temperature, pressure, flow)
Benefits from lowering the supply temperature with iGRID T-
Zone:
• Reduced heat losses – typically 20-30% 
• Improved capacity in the main grid when return temperature is 
lowered
• Possibility of connecting renewable energy sources 
• Reduction of carbon emissions
• Better temperature control resulting in better grid performance
• Higher efficiency in the production plant due to lower return 
temperatures
• Lower pressure loss  in main part of the grid
• Better pressure control when combined with a booster pump

iGRID Building Bypass
- How It Works
•
The motorised bypass valve controls the flow according to temperature 
setpoint vs. data measured continuously by the integrated monitoring device. 
•
Setpoint can be adjusted remotely via the Grundfos iGRID web portal
•
Building Bypass monitors the pressure (flow and return) and temperature (at 
the bypass and e.g. in the return)
•
Logged temperatures and pressures can be transmitted via onboard GSM 
interface in pre-defined intervals to Grundfos Cloud. 
•
Powered from 230V electrical supply on site
•
Data can be accessed via Grundfos iGRID website, or transmitted into SCADA 
systems by means of additional Grundfos iGRID Receiver
•
In buildings with periodic operation (e.g schools) the bypass valve can be 
closed completely during periods with no need for heating.
iGRID Building Bypass – Benefits:
• Reduced return temperature compared to standard bypasses due to continuous 
regulation based  programmed setpoint
• Secured comfort and efficiency at the consumers
• Reduced risk of legionella
• No need to connect to power supply or batteries (Bypass Cabinet)
• Act as “eyes” in the grid

System optimization
Upgrading 
district heating 
in a Chinese city 
Rapid urbanization pressures district heating 
networks. Upgrading and optimizing old 
systems enhances energy efficiency and 
ensures reliable, comfortable heating for 
customers.
The challenge
In the age of rapid urbanization, a major Chinese city struggled 
to meet the increasing heating demand from local customers.
The solution
After conducting a detailed analysis, we replaced their original 
pump system with a prefabricated pump system with remote 
monitoring and a smart control system that enhances heating 
efficiency and stability.
Technical insights
A microcomputer controls the pump system, adjusting 
pressure and flow based on real-time demand and weather, 
ensuring optimal efficiency and consistent heating quality.
The outcome
•
7.2% heat savings through flow optimization
•
4.9% decrease in water loss through pipe leakages
•
8.53% pump energy savings through pumping system optimization

Pressure and temperature zones
Low-temperature 
heating transforms 
Danish districts
Lowering system pressure and temperature 
improves reliability, energy efficiency and 
heat loss while enabling the use of 
sustainable energy and surplus heat.
The challenge
Several Danish district heating providers sought to lower 
outgoing temperatures from 80-100°C to 60-65°C in residential 
zones, but faced challenges due to older networks supplying 
higher than necessary temperatures.
The solution
The solution was Grundfos iGRID Temperature Zone, an off-the-
shelf solution that makes it easy to introduce low-temperature 
zones across district heating networks. iGRID can adjust and 
provide flexible temperatures to meet consumer needs in real 
time, lowering heat loss and costs. 
Technical insights
Grundfos can supply prefabricated Grundfos iGRID units. iGRID includes a digital 
customer platform and pre-engineered Temperature Zones. These come with 
intelligent algorithms that secure precise temperature adjustments.
The outcome
•
Estimated 50% reduction in heat losses
•
A future-proof solution that lets the grid connect to low-temperature 
sources like renewables and surplus heat
•
Quick return on investment, as low as 3.5 years

Intelligent heat 
management using
Grundfos iGRID in 
Gdynia, Poland
Lowering system temperature, makes the system future-
ready, as the lower temperature conditions make it possible 
to connect to low temperature sources such as renewables 
and surplus heat.
The challenge
OPEC Gdynia, a regional local district heating operator in northern Poland, was faced 
with two pressing external challenges. The first, a 
commitment by Gdynia’s mayor to reduce the city’s carbon footprint by 43% by 2030; 
the second, concerns over energy price and supply 
in the region due to the ongoing geopolitical situation.
The solution
Grundfos supplied a prefabricated skid complete with the iGRID Temperature 
Zone solution including:
• iGRID Temperature Optimiser
• Grundfos CU352 V5 control unit
• Grundfos CRE pump with integrated frequency converter
• Weather station
• Flow meter
Technical insights
Grundfos can supply prefabricated Grundfos iGRID units. iGRID includes a digital 
customer platform and pre-engineered Temperature Zones. These come with 
intelligent algorithms that secure precise temperature adjustments.
The outcome
•
Estimated 984GJ reduction of heat transmission losses and energy reduction
•
OPEC was able to achieve ‘White Certificate’. 
These government-issued financial instruments are commodities that can be traded on the Polish Power Exchange; companies 
like OPEC can then use the resultant additional income as a mechanism to encourage further investment in modernisation and 
energy efficiency projects.
•
Quick return on investment, as low as 3.5 years
