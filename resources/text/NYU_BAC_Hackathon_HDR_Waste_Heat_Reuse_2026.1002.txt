Limited power availability remains the prime inhibitor of global data 
center growth in certain core hub markets, leading to opportunities in new 
hotspots like Richmond (North America), Santiago (Latin America) and

emerging hubs. The global weighted average data center vacancy rate fell

markets like São Paulo (–20.8%) and Santiago (–13.7%) had the biggest

•
AI-related training workloads and high-density compute deployments are 
fueling multi-megawatt demand across Tokyo, Sydney and secondary 
hubs like Bogotá and Mumbai. Operators are quickly adopting liquid

Energy Reuse Effectiveness (ERE) is a sustainability metric used 
primarily in data centers to measure how effectively the facility 
repurposes waste heat. It accounts for the energy successfully 
redirected for beneficial uses (like heating nearby buildings) and 
subtracts it from the total facility energy used
Ideal Values: An ERE will always be ≥ 1.0. An ERE of exactly 1.0 
would mean the facility perfectly offsets its infrastructure energy 
costs with energy reuse, though this is practically impossible 
due to thermodynamics and baseline requirements.
Versus PUE: Because ERE factors out reused energy, a facility's 
ERE is always lower than or equal to its standard PUE
Ideal Score: 0.0 kg/kWh, which means the data center 
operates entirely on carbon-free, renewable energy.
Interpretation: A lower CUE score means a lower 
carbon footprint and a more environmentally friendly 
facility.

Carbon & Energy Reuse Metrics | LEGACY
ERE EXAMPLE
Consider a data center located in St. Louis:
IT Equipment Energy: 1000 kWh
Total Facility Energy: 1300 kWh (This means the standard PUE is 
1.3)
Reused Energy: By capturing the exhaust heat from the server 
hot aisles in the winter, the facility redirects 200 kWh of thermal 
energy to heat an adjacent office building.
What this means: In traditional efficiency metrics, the facility had 
a PUE of 1.3. However, because it actively recycled 200 kWh of 
heat, its effective energy overhead was drastically reduced, 
resulting in an ERE of 1.1.

Air Cooling | Traditional (Legacy) Cooling
For decades, computer rooms and data centers utilized raised floor systems to deliver cold air to servers. Cold air from a computer 
room air conditioner (CRAC) or computer room air handler (CRAH) pressurized the space below the raised floor. Perforated tiles 
provided a means for the cold air to leave the plenum and enter the main space—ideally in front of server intakes. After passing 
through the server, the heated air returned to the CRAC/CRAH to be cooled, usually after mixing with the cold air. Most commonly the 
CRAC unit fans ran at a constant speed, and the CRAC had a humidifier within the unit that produced steam. The primary benefit of a 
raised floor, from a cooling standpoint, is to deliver cold air where it is needed, with very little effort, by simply swapping a solid tile for 
a perforated tile.


--- page 32 ---
Air Cooling | Hot Aisle Containment (HAC)
This method encloses the hot aisles within the data center. The heat discharged from the racks enters the hot aisle and is returned 
through ductworks or a plenum back to the cooling equipment. The cold air supply floods the rest of the data center through raised 
floor perforated tiles or overhead via ductwork.
Cold Aisle Containment (CAC)


--- page 33 ---
Air Cooling | Close-Coupled Cooling
This moves the cooling unit closer to the IT equipment rack rather than relying on air plenums or ducting, reducing the fan power 
required and increasing efficiency. This is done with in-row or rear-door coolers, which place the cooling units in the equipment rows 
between the IT equipment racks.
Rear-Door Cooling (example: RDHx)
In rack-oriented cooling architectures, the CRAC units are dedicated to 
one server rack and are directly mounted to (or within) the racks. 
Compared with the room-oriented or row-oriented architecture, the rack-
oriented airflow paths are even shorter and more precisely defined, so the 
highest power density (up to 60 kW per rack) can be achieved. 
In-Row Cooling
With a row-oriented cooling architecture, each CRAC unit is dedicated to cooling 
one row of server racks.  A row-oriented design also allows cooling capacity and 
redundancy to be targeted to the actual needs of specific rows. For example, row-
oriented architecture allows one row of racks to run high density applications such 
as blade servers, while another row satisfies lower power density applications 
such as communication enclosures.


--- page 34 ---
Liquid Cooling | Single-Phase Direct-to-Chip Cooling
A direct-to-chip (D2C) cooling system replaces traditional air fans with a liquid-based closed loop. It extracts heat by circulating a 
coolant through cold plates mounted directly to high-density processors (CPUs/GPUs). A Cooling Distribution Unit (CDU) is 
introduced between the primary cooling plant and piping distribution in the server hall. The absorbed heat is then pumped out of the 
CDU for facility-wide rejection.


--- page 35 ---
Liquid Cooling | Immersion Cooling
Immersion cooling is an advanced thermal management technique where electronic equipment, such as data center servers, is 
directly submerged in a thermally conductive, non-conductive (dielectric) liquid. By eliminating fans and heat sinks, it maximizes 
energy efficiency, allows for higher component density, and reduces the water consumption often required in traditional air-cooled 
facilities.
Two-Phase
Single-Phase
Low boiling point liquids are chosen, so the heat generated by servers can easily cause 
a phase change where the liquid surrounding the components boils and generates 
vapor, which in turn goes through a phase change returning it to a liquid state while 
removing the heat. After changing phases, as the bubbles rise up through the liquid the 
vapor is condensed on the sealed tank's condenser coils, where it changes from a 
gaseous phase back to a liquid phase and returns the fluid to the immersion bath. 
Heat generated by servers is conducted through the direct contact between the 
medium and components. Unlike two-phase liquid immersion cooling, this type 
of system requires a pump in the coolant distribution unit (CDU) to suck the 
medium into the cooling device for cooling and goes a step further by using an 
additional heat rejection equipment to complete the heat exchange cycle.


--- page 36 ---
Liquid Cooling | Two-Phase Direct-to-Chip Cooling
Two-phase direct-to-chip cooling is an advanced thermal management technique where a specialized dielectric fluid flows over a 
chip, absorbs heat, and boils. The resulting vapor carries the heat away to a condenser, drastically reducing the required fluid volume 
while efficiently cooling high-intensity hardware.
Benefits:
•
Higher Efficiency: Requires 4 to 9 times less fluid flow rate compared to single-phase water solutions, reducing pumping power 
requirements.
•
Superior Cooling Capacity: Capable of dissipating extreme heat fluxes (well over 2000 W per chip) without thermal throttling.
•
Safety & Uniformity: Boiling occurs at a constant saturation temperature, providing uniform cooling across the whole chip. 
Because it uses dielectric liquids instead of water, the risk of short-circuiting equipment during a leak is vastly reduced.

Networks (TENs) and how to optimize the network/loop design for thermal balance, land allocation, and energy 
efficiency/decarbonization potential.  The INFOGRAPHIC below is an illustration of an optimized TEN that allows for

addition to buildings of varying function types being inter-connected to the TEN, the network also includes thermal 
balancing elements including a centralized Geothermal/GeoExchange borefield, Wastewater Energy Recovery, and

Carbonate Fuel Cells | Waste Heat Reuse 
Data center cooling can be improved (and energy consumption reduced) by using on-site carbonate fuel cell combined heat and power 
(CHP) to drive absorption chilling—converting fuel cell waste heat into chilled water so that less electricity is spent on cooling. FuelCell 
Energy Block systems produce a single high-flow exhaust stream at 725 deg F that can generate hot water, steam, or chilled 
water via an absorption chiller, turning thermal output into a data-center-scale cooling asset and freeing more electrical capacity for IT 
load. By capturing and using this heat, overall system efficiency can exceed 80%, delivering significant energy savings and lower 
emissions compared with conventional electric cooling.
